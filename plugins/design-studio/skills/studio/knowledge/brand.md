# Brand identity and marketing design

> Used by: brand designer (owner), visual designer, content strategist, emotion designer. Distilled from: Designing Brand Identity (Wheeler); The Brand Gap and Zag (Neumeier); How Brands Grow (Sharp) and Ehrenberg-Bass work on distinctive assets (Romaniuk); Designing for Emotion (Walter); Laws of UX (Yablonski); Anthropic's frontend-design skill; Craft by Gustavo Fior (how to get references, timelessness); Anshu Chimala (Lenny's Newsletter); world-class-designer (Lammertsma); design-director (Prunty) bench notes. Written in our own words; see CREDITS.md.

Use this to build an identity out of the subject's own world and to write the page that sells it. Visual execution lives in `kb/knowledge/visual-systems.md` and words in `kb/knowledge/content.md`; this file decides what the brand is and how it argues. Principles generalise; Values are defaults to beat, not ceilings. Judge in context; never apply mechanically. One test runs under every principle: could this identity be put on a competitor without anyone noticing? If yes, it is not finished.

## 1. Build the identity from the subject's world: Subject grounding (frontend-design)

Distinctive identities come from the subject's industry, materials, tools, places and vernacular, never from the last project or a UI kit (visual application: `kb/knowledge/visual-systems.md` #1).

- Before any visual decision, list ten concrete nouns from the subject's world (materials, instruments, documents, places, rituals) and three words insiders use that outsiders don't.
- Draw the mark, the owned colour, the type voice, the imagery and the signature move from that list.
- Give every client an identity that could not be mistaken for anyone else's, including our own past clients.
- Do not start from a component kit, a favourite palette or "modern and clean".

Check: a critic can name at least two things in the first screenshot that come from the subject's world; with a competitor's name swapped in, the page should no longer fit.

## 2. Find the one thing only this brand can say: Onliness (Neumeier)

Neumeier treats a brand as the gut feeling people hold about a company, and focus is what makes that feeling specific, so write the sentence no competitor could sign.

- Use the form "<Brand> is the only <category> that <difference>", then add for whom, where and why now.
- If it needs "and" or a second sentence, it is not focused yet; cut until it doesn't.
- When the category zigs, zag: choose the difference rivals could not copy without becoming someone else.
- Do not position on claims every competitor makes (fast, simple, secure, powerful, all-in-one).

Check: the positioning line in the brief fails the substitution test for each named competitor; the hero headline states the difference, not the category.

## 3. Be recognisable before being persuasive: Distinctive brand assets (Sharp; Romaniuk)

People rarely remember positioning but they do recognise assets, so a brand grows by being easy to notice and recall: a colour, a shape, a wordmark, a type voice, a phrase, a sound.

- Pick two or three assets that are unique in the category and tie them to the brand name everywhere.
- Build mental availability: link the brand, in plain words, to the buying situations in which people think of the category.
- Judge an asset on uniqueness (no rival uses it) and fame (people tie it to us); invest only in assets that can win both.
- Do not swap the palette, tagline or mark with each launch; recognition is built by repetition.

Check: the same two or three assets appear on every surface in the screenshot set (header, empty state, success, email, OG tile) and in none of the competitor screenshots.

## 4. Choose consistency over novelty: Consistency over time (Sharp; Wheeler)

Distinctiveness compounds only when it is used the same way for years, so fix the core and let expression flex around it.

- Fix the core as tokens: wordmark, owned colour, type families, the signature move.
- Flex the expression: layouts, image subjects, campaign lines.
- New surfaces inherit the core; they do not get a new look because they are new.
- Do not mistake sameness for a lack of ideas; consistency is a creative discipline.

Check: measure.json shows the same type families on every screen; the brand hex and the wordmark treatment are identical across the set.
Values: two type families at most across the brand (studio default).

## 5. Run the identity process in order: Identity process (Wheeler)

Identity work runs research, strategy, design, touchpoints, then asset management, and skipping to the logo produces decoration with no reason.

- Research: the audience, the competitors' identities, the subject's world, the user's references.
- Strategy: positioning line and attributes agreed before any visual exploration.
- Design two or three directions, each traced to the strategy; then the touchpoints; then the rules that keep it consistent.
- Do not draw the mark first and back-fill a strategy to justify it.

Check: direction.md states positioning and attributes above any palette; each style tile names the attribute it expresses.

## 6. Pair every attribute with its near miss: Attributes and anti-attributes (Walter; Neumeier)

An adjective alone describes every brand, but an attribute paired with the trait it must not slide into shows the team where the edge is.

- Write three to five pairs: "precise, not cold", "warm, not cute", "confident, not loud".
- Give each pair a visible consequence: a token, a rule, a forbidden move.
- Add anti-attributes: what the brand must never look or sound like, including the user's anti-inspirations and the nearest generated cluster.
- Do not use "modern", "clean", "innovative" or "premium" as attributes; they decide nothing.

Check: for each attribute, a critic can point to its consequence in the screenshots; a blind critic can guess two attributes from the first screen.

## 7. Refuse the generated-identity defaults: Slop clusters (frontend-design)

Generated identities cluster around a few looks and lines that appear whatever the subject, and each reads as a default rather than a choice (visual list and replacements: `kb/knowledge/visual-systems.md` #29).

- Visual clusters: cream with serif and terracotta; near-black with one acid accent; broadsheet hairlines; the SaaS-card kit; template chrome.
- Brand-level clusters: a gradient-blob or sparkle mark, a name ending in -ly or -ify, "the all-in-one platform for", a hero of smiling stock teams, the canonical landing stack.
- Use a cluster only when the brief asks for it, then execute it better than the default; otherwise replace it with a choice from the subject list (principle 1).
- Do not swap one cluster for another (cream to near-black) and call it a new direction.

Check: compare the first screenshot and the H1 against both lists; any match without a brief reason is a finding with its replacement named.

## 8. Find references outside the discipline: How to get references (Craft: how-to-get-references)

References taken from other interfaces drag work toward the average of the feed, while film, architecture, print, music and physical objects carry mechanisms nobody in the category is using.

- Collect from outside UI: a film's staging, a building's threshold, a timetable's grid, a tool's detent.
- Translate, do not transplant: name the mechanism (how it directs attention, builds tension, leaves things out) and rebuild it in our medium.
- Write the translation down; critics shown photographs as moodboards dismissed them almost every time (31 of 33 in the design-director bench), but they act on written rules.
- Do not copy a reference's surface; if you cannot say what it taught without showing it, it taught nothing.

Check: the reference brief lists at least three references, two from outside UI, each with one line naming the mechanism; that mechanism is visible in a style tile.

## 9. Keep what will survive the trend: Timelessness (Craft: timelessness)

Surface ages fast and structure slowly, so recognition should rest on proportion, type and colour relationships rather than on this year's effect.

- Ask of each effect: would this still make sense if the trend around it vanished tomorrow?
- Borrow from a wave only what you can justify without pointing at what others shipped.
- Prefer honest materials that belong to the subject over glass and glow the platform does not have.
- Do not anchor the brand on an effect (frosted glass, mesh gradients, long shadows); effects are what carbon-date a brand.

Check: in a greyscale view with effects ignored, the first screenshot still reads as this brand.

## 10. Make the mark work at its smallest first: Logo and wordmark (Wheeler)

The eye takes in shape first, then colour, then reads, so a mark must be recognisable by silhouette and colour before anyone reads it.

- Draw for 16px first: the favicon and app icon decide the silhouette; larger sizes add detail.
- Kern the wordmark by hand and alter one or two letters with intent, rather than typing the name in a bold weight of a stock face.
- Specify a one-colour version, a reversed version, a minimum size and clear space.
- Do not bolt a generic symbol (spark, hexagon, swoosh, gradient blob) onto a wordmark.

Check: the system sheet shows the mark at 16, 32, 64 and 160px on light, dark and one colour, legible at 16px; blurred, the first screenshot is still recognisable by shape and colour.
Values: clear space at least the cap height; favicon 16 and 32px plus SVG; app icon 1024px with a safe area.

## 11. Own a colour, not a rainbow: Colour as asset (Sharp; Wheeler)

One owned hue used consistently is among the strongest assets a brand can have; a palette spread everywhere is no asset at all.

- Choose one brand colour that is unusual in the category and drawn from the subject's world, and define where it may appear.
- Derive neutrals from the subject's materials (paper, stone, steel, soil) rather than default greys.
- Agree roles and contrast pairs with @visual-designer: the brand colour is either the accent or a field, by rule, not at random.
- Do not default to blue-to-purple gradients or a new accent per section.

Check: one brand hue across all screens with an identical hex; on working screens the accent stays small (roughly 10% of the first viewport) unless the brief makes it a field.

## 12. Let type carry the voice: Typography voice (frontend-design)

Typography carries most of a page's personality, so choose families for this brief and use display type as an active part of the identity.

- Use one family, or two that are clearly distinct, drawn from the subject's era and vernacular.
- Treat the headline setting (scale, weight, width, line breaks) as part of the identity; agree families, scale and numerals with @typographer.
- Name the type voice in two or three words that match the attributes ("engineered, dry, exact").
- Do not accent a single headline word with italic, bold or colour, and do not make tracked all-caps labels the default.

Check: measure.json lists two families at most; no headline has one word in a different colour or style.

## 13. Write the page as an argument: Marketing narrative (studio)

A marketing page moves a reader through problem, promise, proof, offer and action, and each section exists to create one belief.

- List, in order, the beliefs a buyer must hold before acting: this is my problem; this solves it; it works for people like me; it is worth the price; it is safe to try.
- Make one section per belief, with a heading that states the belief rather than a label.
- For a short page, use the plain formula: what it is, what it does, one reassurance, one proof point, one call to action.
- Do not default to the canonical stack (hero, logo wall, three features, how it works, testimonials, pricing, FAQ, CTA) without testing it against the belief list.

Check: the measure.json headings outline reads as a sequence of claims a reader could agree with; no H2 is a bare label such as "Features" or "Testimonials".

## 14. Prove claims with real evidence only: Real proof (studio)

Proof persuades only when it is true and specific, and invented proof is both a tell and a liability.

- Use real numbers with a source, real customers with permission, and real product screenshots at true scale.
- Place each piece of proof beside the claim it supports.
- Leave a slot empty and mark it `TODO(user)` rather than fill it with placeholder logos or quotes.
- Do not write "Trusted by 10,000+ teams", star ratings or testimonials without a real source.

Check: every number and quote in the screenshots is product data or carries its source; no logo wall the user has not confirmed.

## 15. Make the hero one idea and one action: The hero (frontend-design; Hayes)

The hero is the first thing anyone sees, so it opens with the most characteristic thing in the subject's world and asks for one action.

- Choose the form deliberately: a headline, an image, a live demo, an object, an interactive moment.
- Put one idea in the headline, in the buyer's words; the subhead adds how or for whom and never restates it.
- Offer one primary CTA and at most one quiet secondary.
- Do not reach for the big number with small label and gradient, or text left with graphic right, unless it is truly the best form.

Check: at 1280×800 and 390×844 the first viewport holds the headline, the primary CTA and the hero subject; two CTAs at most; H1 of about 10 words or fewer.
Values: H1 up to 10 words; subhead up to 25 words (studio defaults).

## 16. Spend boldness in one place, every time: The signature move (frontend-design; Vmobify)

Pick one signature move, not five: one memorable element against a calm field is remembered, and repeated across surfaces it becomes an asset (visual execution: `kb/knowledge/visual-systems.md` #3).

- Derive the move from the subject list and the positioning: a type treatment, an image, an interaction, a layout break, a material.
- Place it once per surface, where the most important idea lives; keep everything around it quiet.
- Carry the same move across touchpoints (hero, empty state, email, OG tile) so it compounds into recognition.
- Do not scatter effects; before shipping, remove one accessory.

Check: a critic can name the signature move from the first screenshot in one phrase; naming two, or none, is a finding.

## 17. Make the one important thing different: Von Restorff effect (Yablonski)

The item that differs from its surroundings is noticed and remembered, but only against a uniform field (general law: `kb/knowledge/ux-laws.md` #7).

- Reserve the strongest contrast for the primary action or the signature move.
- Differ by more than colour (size, shape, position, weight) so the effect survives without colour vision.
- Keep the field calm; isolation fails when everything is emphasised.
- Do not spend the accent on decoration.

Check: count accent-coloured elements in the first viewport; the most prominent one is the primary CTA or the signature move.

## 18. Use polish to earn patience, not to hide problems: Aesthetic-usability effect (Kurosu and Kashimura; Yablonski)

People find attractive designs easier to use and forgive small faults, which is an asset in the product and a trap in reviews.

- Invest finish where first impressions form: the hero, first run, the signature move.
- Keep the product screens and states at the hero's level of finish; the marketing page must not promise what the product does not keep.
- Remember the patience runs out when polish dates; finish rests on structure (principle 9).
- Do not let beauty excuse a broken flow; usability findings outrank it.

Check: product screens and states share the hero's type, colour and finish; the state board is scored on its own, apart from the hero.

## 19. Give every image a job: Imagery with purpose (world-class-designer; Chimala)

An image earns its place by doing what words cannot: showing the product, setting the material, establishing a space, carrying the mood or showing a change of state.

- Name each image's job in a few words before making it.
- Make images as a set from one style stem (medium, era, light, palette, background) reused verbatim, changing only the subject; brief @art-director when present.
- Integrate them: blend into the ground, mask, set type into the image, grade to the palette.
- Do not use stock teams, isometric people packs, abstract 3D blobs or screenshots tilted in device frames.

Check: for each image in the screenshots, a critic can state its job; all images share palette, light and medium.

## 20. Replace stand-in gradients with real imagery: Generated imagery (Chimala: technique 4)

Coding agents reach for gradients, blobs and shapes where a designer would use an image, and that substitution is one of the plainest signs of generated work.

- When the hero has no object to look at, or a gradient fills the space an image should, generate an image.
- Brief it like a photographer: one subject, medium and era, composition with room for text, light, palette hex, exclusions (no text, no logos).
- Generate several for the hero and pick; log every prompt so a matching asset can be made later.
- Without image generation, draw SVG for the direction, use real texture, or let type be the image; never orbs and glows.

Check: no large gradient, blob or glow stands in for content in the first viewport. Check (code): each generated asset has its prompt logged in the design record.

## 21. Make every touchpoint part of the identity: Touchpoints (Wheeler)

An identity is judged at its weakest touchpoint, which is rarely the hero.

- List them: favicon, app icon, OG image, 404, empty states, loading, errors, transactional email, receipts, docs.
- Give each the core assets and the voice; none may fall back to default styling.
- Design them before calling the identity done.
- Do not leave the 404 and the emails to framework defaults.

Check: the system sheet and state board include the favicon, a 1200×630 OG tile, the 404 and one email header, all in the brand.
Values: OG image 1200×630; favicon 16 and 32px plus SVG.

## 22. Fix the brand architecture early: Brand architecture (Wheeler)

Whether the company is one branded house, endorses sub-brands or runs a house of brands decides how products and features are named and shown.

- Choose monolithic, endorsed or pluralistic and record it in direction.md.
- Apply one naming pattern to every product and feature.
- Carry the masterbrand's assets onto every sub-brand unless the architecture says otherwise.
- Do not invent a sub-brand per feature; most features want plain descriptive names.

Check: product and feature names in the headings outline and navigation follow one pattern.

## 23. Name things to be said aloud: Naming (Neumeier)

A good brand name is distinctive, brief, appropriate, easy to spell and say, likeable, extendable and protectable; feature names are a different job and should simply describe.

- Test brand names out loud, in a sentence, in a search box and as a domain.
- Name features by what users do with them ("Invoices", "Reminders"), not by internal codenames.
- Set the naming rules; @content-strategist writes the labels and microcopy.
- Do not coin playful names for ordinary features.

Check: navigation labels in the screenshots are plain nouns a user would search for; no feature label needs a tooltip to be understood.

## 24. Keep voice and visuals telling the same story: Voice alignment (Walter)

Voice stays constant while tone flexes with the moment, and both must match what the visuals promise.

- Derive the voice from the same attribute pairs as the visuals: a precise identity writes short and numeric; a warm one writes plain and generous.
- Set tone by moment with @content-strategist and @emotion-designer: calm in errors, brief in success, specific in proof.
- Read the strings in a row (hero, CTA, empty, error, success); they should sound like one person.
- Do not let a restrained identity shout in exclamation marks, or a playful one speak legal prose.

Check: five strings sampled from the screenshots (hero, CTA, empty, error, success) all fit the attribute pairs, and none uses the banned words in `kb/knowledge/ai-tells.md`.

## When these conflict

Within brand: the positioning beats a pretty idea that does not serve it; the fixed core beats a fresh look for a new surface; real proof beats a fuller page; one signature move beats a second bold idea; Sharp's distinctiveness governs execution (be recognisable, consistently) while Neumeier's difference governs what we say. The user's references set the level; these principles set the floor. Then defer to the studio ladder:

1. Accessibility and legibility floor
2. Explicit user decisions
3. Clarity
4. User control
5. Task completion
6. System consistency
7. Brand distinctiveness
8. Delight
